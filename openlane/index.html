01. Context

Why the car flow worked so well

OPENLANE is a wholesale marketplace where dealers buy and sell used vehicles. To list a car, the dealer scans the VIN in the app. The app then auto-populates the year, make, model and trim in seconds.

This works well for cars because:

  • Standardized 17-character identifiers
  • Printed on labels or dashboards
  • Barcode scanning and OCR (text scan) decode and auto-populate accurately

02. The problem

Stand in the lot for a minute

Seven in the morning, early March in Ontario, −6°C (21°F). Powersports sales peak in summer, so the dealer needs eleven vehicles listed before then. The first is a dirt bike with mud dried hard on the frame.

They tap to list it and the camera asks for the VIN. On a car, this step is already done. Here the number is stamped into the frame and half packed with dirt.

Unable to scan. Another angle, then the flashlight. Nothing typed, no photos, no price. The phone goes back in the pocket, and the bike loses another day before summer.

Core insight

"Scan" is three different jobs

A scan looks like one action, but it's really three separate steps: capture, decode and match. Each one can work or fail on its own. Seeing them separately changed the question from "did the scan work?" to "what do we do when one step comes up empty?" That question shaped the whole design.

1 · Capture

Read the
characters

  • Turns the number into text
  • Always works once the dealer has the number
2 · Decode

Work out
the vehicle

  • Gets year, make and model from the number itself
  • Only works for standard VINs
3 · Match

Look the
number up

  • Searches a database for past records and value
  • Many powersports units aren't in it

The old flow asked the dealer to succeed at the hardest task first.
The redesign assumes that task will sometimes fail and makes failure a non-event.

04. Research & feasibility

Pressure-testing what's actually possible

Before designing anything "smart", I worked with engineering to check what the backend could actually return. Two findings shaped the design.

Finding 1 · Identifiers

Not every ID can be decoded

  • Most motorcycles and ATVs have a 17-character VIN that decodes fully
  • Watercraft use a HINHull Identification Number: the ID stamped on boats and personal watercraft., which only gives the maker and build date
  • Snowmobiles and older units often have plain serials with nothing to decode
Finding 2 · Valuation data

Many units won't find a match

  • Pricing data for powersports is thinner than for cars
  • Watercraft are often missing altogether
  • So "no match" is common, not rare
◆ The constraint that shaped the UI

Since most units won't match, I designed "nothing came back" as the calm, expected default, not an error. A clean decode or match is a bonus on top.

05. Product strategy

Four rules I held the design to

Rule 01

Never dead-end

  • Every failure has a way forward
  • Manual entry is always one tap away
Rule 02

One flow,
shaped by type

  • Where to find the VIN
  • Which photos to take (per vehicle type)
Rule 03

Data helps, but never blocks

  • Found data fills in the details for the dealer
  • No data? The dealer carries on as normal
Rule 04

Say what's wrong and how to fix it

  • Plain reasons, like "the label looks dirty"
  • A next step, like "wipe the label"
  • Never just "failed"

Pick the vehicle type first

The dealer picks the vehicle type first, so the app asks for the right ID and shows where to find it.

Help is always one tap away

While scanning, the dealer is never stuck.

  • “Where’s the VIN?” stays on the scan screen
  • Manual entry is available at any point
  • Help shows up before an error, not after
0:00

When a scan fails, help steps up

A failed scan isn’t a dead end. Each miss offers a bit more help.

  • First miss: a gentle nudge
  • Next: name the cause and what to do
  • After 3 tries: suggest manual entry
  • The camera never turns off
Agreed with engineering

The camera reads the VIN many times a second, so one read isn’t one try.

A scan only counts as failed if the VIN can’t be read clearly, or after 5 seconds with no match.

09. Reflection

The biggest lever wasn't a screen. It was a reframe.

Treating "scan" as three jobs instead of one turned a scary, all-or-nothing failure into a set of small, designable states.

01

Design for real life,
not the perfect case.

"No match" is common in powersports, so it had to feel normal, not like an error.

02

Set expectations
at the start.

A separate powersports entry told dealers upfront this isn’t the car flow, so a VIN that doesn’t auto-fill feels expected, not broken.

03

Feasibility is a
design input.

Knowing early what the data could return kept the design realistic.

Where I'd take it next

Track where scans fail for each vehicle type, then tune the retry rules. Confirm which price data covers powersports in Canada. And show live hints, like “too dark” or “hold steady”, so dealers can fix the shot before a scan fails.
